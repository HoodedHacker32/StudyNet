About StudyNet, and how AI is used

StudyNet is free revision for Junior Cycle, with no accounts and no ads. We think you should know exactly how it was made and where AI is involved, so here it is.

Who made it

StudyNet was designed by a Junior Cycle student. The first version of the site was built from that design by Claude Sonnet 4.6, an AI model made by Anthropic.

This version, with the shared design, 2nd Year content, Maths, practice exams and marking, was built with Claude Opus 5.5, also by Anthropic. The student made the design decisions, tested everything and checked the results.

Where the content comes from

  • Topics and notes come from what teachers set and wrote for each class: the November assessment topics and the class notes. Claude Opus 5.5 turned those notes into the notes pages, flashcards, quizzes, matching games, timelines and fill-in practice you see here.
  • Gaeilge words, spelling and grammar were checked against the dictionaries at teanglann.ie and focloir.ie. The story Spás isn’t copied here: StudyNet links to it in the Turas 3 textbook online.
  • Maths notes follow the methods taught in class. Maths practice questions are made by code, and every answer is checked by code, not AI.
  • The calculator’s screen uses the free font ClassWiz X Display by Windy Deng (SIL Open Font License). StudyNet isn’t connected with any calculator maker.
  • Practice exam questions are exam-style questions written by Claude Opus 5.5, modelled on class tests and on the kinds of question that come up again and again in recent Junior Cycle papers. They are written in StudyNet’s own words and are not real past-paper questions. Real past papers and marking schemes are free on examinations.ie (opens in a new tab).

AI can get things wrong, even when its work has been checked. If something in a note or an answer looks wrong, check with your teacher. Your teacher is always right over StudyNet.

How AI is used while you study

Two things, both about checking your work.

Marking. When you finish a practice exam, AI marks your written answers and your maths working against that question’s marking scheme, point by point, and tells you which points you got. The mark is the total of those points.

Checking your French and Irish answers. In the Français and Gaeilge exam run-throughs, you can ask StudyNet’s checker to look at a paragraph you wrote (typed, or a photo of your copy). It points out mistakes, with a fix for each one, and says what your teacher asked for that’s still missing. It never writes or rewrites your paragraph for you. A quick check for common mistakes runs first, using simple rules and no AI.

What’s sent
For marking: the question, its marking scheme, a sample answer and your answer (plus a photo of your working, if you add one). For a paragraph check: your paragraph (or its photo) and your teacher’s checklist. Never your name, profile or scores.
Where it goes
StudyNet’s marking server, which uses Groq (or Cloudflare Workers AI as a backup) to run the model. Everyone’s papers are marked the same way.
Is it kept?
StudyNet doesn’t store your answers. Everything you do is saved in your own browser only.
What if it isn’t working?
If the marker can’t be reached (for example, you’re offline), you mark your own written answers by ticking the marking-scheme points you got. The quick check on your French and Irish answers still works offline.

What AI doesn’t do

  • It doesn’t write questions while you use StudyNet. Every question comes from the fixed question banks.
  • It doesn’t decide maths answers. Code checks those, and AI can never give full marks for a maths answer the code says is wrong. It can only give method marks for correct working.
  • It doesn’t write your paragraphs. It only points out mistakes in what you wrote.
  • There’s no AI chat, and nothing tracks you.
  • Short answers and multiple choice are marked by simple code, not AI.

Spelling when marking

In Science, History, Geography and Maths, spelling isn’t what’s being tested, so an answer counts if it’s readable or sounds right when read aloud: “nucleas” counts for “nucleus”. A different real word never counts: “neuron” isn’t “neutron”. In Gaeilge and Français, spelling and accents count where the marking scheme asks for them, because they are part of the language.
